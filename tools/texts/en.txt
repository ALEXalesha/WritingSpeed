Why is the sky blue? Sunlight looks white, but it is a mix of all the colors of the rainbow. As it passes through the air, light meets tiny gas molecules, and they scatter blue light much more strongly than red. This scattered blue light reaches us from every part of the sky, so during the day the sky looks blue. At sunset the sun's rays have to travel through a much thicker layer of air. How does a rainbow work? Sunlight is made of rays of different colors, which together look white to us. When millions of drops hang in the air after rain, each of them becomes a tiny prism. A ray of light enters a drop, bends, reflects off its back wall and comes out again. Different colors bend by slightly different angles: red less, violet more. So from each drop only one color reaches your eye, and together all the drops form a colored arc. You can see a rainbow only when the sun is behind you and the rain is in front of you. The lower the sun, the higher the arc, so the most beautiful rainbows appear in the morning and toward evening. Sometimes a second, paler rainbow appears above the main one. Its colors are in reverse order, because the light reflects twice inside each drop. And the sky between the two arcs looks darker: the drops send almost no light there. You can't touch a rainbow or walk up to it: it is built anew each time for the eyes of whoever is looking. So if you see a rainbow, remember: nobody else in the world sees exactly the same one.

Why do we yawn? For a long time people thought yawning gave us more oxygen. But experiments showed that people yawn just as often whether they breathe ordinary air or air rich in oxygen. Today scientists lean toward another explanation: yawning helps cool the brain. During a deep breath, cool air passes through the nose and mouth, and the jaw muscles boost blood flow to the head. Like a computer, the brain works better when it isn't overheated. That is why we often yawn when we are tired, bored or about to fall asleep, and right after waking up: at those moments the brain needs to switch to a new mode. Interestingly, yawning is contagious. When we see someone else yawn, we are almost sure to yawn ourselves, and sometimes just reading about yawning is enough. This happens most often with people we are close to and feel for. Contagious yawning is found in animals too: in dogs, chimpanzees and even parrots. If you yawned while reading this text, don't worry: it means your brain is fine and you can feel for others. By the way, it is almost impossible to yawn with your mouth closed: try it, and you'll feel your jaw muscles straining to open.

How do plants drink water? They have no heart and no pump, yet water rises from the roots to the leaves even in the tallest trees, higher than a thirty-story building. The secret is evaporation. On the underside of the leaves are tiny openings called stomata. Through them the plant breathes and slowly releases water into the air. As water molecules evaporate from a leaf, they pull their neighbors along, because water molecules cling to one another. So an unbroken thread of water stretches through the finest tubes inside the stem, all the way from the leaves to the roots. Meanwhile the roots draw new water from the soil, along with dissolved minerals. On a hot day a large tree can evaporate hundreds of liters of water. That is why a forest in summer is cooler and more humid than a city: the trees work like huge living air conditioners. When a plant is short of water, its stomata close to save moisture, and its leaves wilt and droop. Water the plant, and within an hour it spreads its leaves again. So every blade of grass is a tiny plumbing system that works without a single moving part. Next time you see a wilted flower on the windowsill, remember how much complex work is going on inside it.

How does memory work? When we learn something, the brain doesn't put that fact on a separate shelf. Instead, the connections between nerve cells grow stronger. Each cell is linked to thousands of others, and a memory is a particular pattern of connections that lights up when we recall something. The more often we return to a memory, the stronger the connections become and the easier it is to find. That is why repetition matters so much for learning. But it is better to repeat with breaks than all at once: today, the next day, in a week, in a month. Each time the brain forgets a little and then remembers again, and the connections get stronger. Scientists call this spaced repetition. Sleep plays an important role too. At night the brain sorts through the events of the day: it moves what matters into long-term memory and throws away the rest. That is why it is hard to study after a sleepless night, and why things learned before bed are often remembered better. Interestingly, memory is not like a video recording. Each time we recall an event, the brain puts it together again from pieces and may change or add something without meaning to. Two people who saw the same thing often describe it differently, and both are being sincere. Memory helps us not only keep the past but also imagine the future: we build plans from the same pieces we use for memories. So training your memory is useful not for remembering everything, but for thinking better. And the best ways to train it are to learn new things, move, sleep well and often tell others what you have learned. If you want to remember something for a long time, try drawing a diagram, coming up with an example from your own life or explaining it to a friend. The more different connections you build around new knowledge, the more paths the brain will have to find its way back to it.

Why is it so easy to find your way in the subway, even though you can't see anything underground? Special designers work on that. Each line gets its own color, and the same color marks the signs, the maps and even the stripes on the walls of the passages. Station names are written in a large, simple typeface that is easy to read on the move and from far away. A subway map is usually not drawn the way the lines really run. The real tunnels bend under streets and rivers, but on the map the lines go straight or at neat angles, and the stations are spaced almost evenly. This kind of map was invented by an engineering draftsman in London almost a hundred years ago. He noticed that passengers care not about distance but about the order of stations and where to change trains, and he drew a map that looked like an electrical circuit diagram. At first his idea was met with doubt, but passengers loved it at once, and today subway maps all over the world are drawn this way. Another trick is the announcements in the cars. In some cities the next station is announced by a male voice when the train is heading toward the center and by a female voice when it is heading away. Blind and visually impaired passengers find their way by sound and by raised stripes on the floor that lead to the exits and warn of the platform edge. Good wayfinding is invisible: when everything is done right, you simply walk and never think about how many people helped you find the way. We notice it only when something is wrong: a sign hangs in the wrong place or an arrow points at a wall. That is why designers test their ideas on real passengers, watching where people stop and look around in confusion. So next time you easily find the right exit, remember that behind that ease are years of work, hundreds of tests and countless details that nobody notices.

Where do clouds come from? Water is always evaporating from seas, rivers and even the ground after rain. Invisible water vapor rises with the warm air. The higher it goes, the colder it gets, and at some height the vapor becomes too cold to stay a gas. It turns into tiny droplets of water or crystals of ice. Each droplet needs something to form on: a speck of dust, a grain of sea salt or ash. Billions of these droplets together make the cloud we see from the ground. Clouds come in many kinds. High cirrus clouds look like thin white feathers and are made of ice crystals. Cumulus clouds, which look like cotton wool, appear on sunny days when warm air rises in columns. If such a cloud grows too big, it turns into a thundercloud: dark, tall, with a flat top like an anvil. Inside a thundercloud, drops and bits of ice rush up and down, collide and build up an electric charge that is later released as lightning. When the drops become heavy enough, they can no longer stay in the air and fall as rain, or as snow when it is freezing. A cloud that looks light can actually weigh hundreds of tons: the water is just spread through a huge volume of air. Watching clouds is not only pleasant but useful. For centuries sailors and farmers predicted the weather from their shape and color. High feathery clouds often arrive a day before the weather changes, and fast-growing white towers on a summer day promise a thunderstorm toward evening. Try writing down one evening what clouds you saw during the day, and in the morning check what the weather turned out to be. In a couple of weeks you will start noticing patterns that old sailors knew. And clouds are simply beautiful: you can spot dragons, ships and whole cities in them, changing right before your eyes.

My first pancake, of course, came out a lumpy mess. The second stuck and tore, the third burned on one side. I was ready to give up, but Grandma said her first try had been no better, and the pancakes would come, just not right away. By the tenth pancake I was getting the hang of it: thin, golden, with lacy edges. Grandma solemnly put it on a separate plate and said it was my first real pancake. We ate the whole stack with sour cream and cherry jam and took the rest to our neighbor, who praised them as if they were a birthday cake. Since then I make pancakes for the whole family every Pancake Day. The pan lives with me now, and I still check the batter by eye. Sometimes the first pancake still turns out a mess, and every time I smile, remembering that Saturday. Recently I taught my little brother to make pancakes. His first one was a mess too, and I told him the same words Grandma once told me.

How does a satellite stay in orbit without falling to Earth? It seems that if gravity is pulling on it, it should fall sooner or later. In fact, a satellite really is falling all the time, but it is moving sideways so fast that it keeps missing the Earth. Imagine throwing a stone from a high mountain. The harder you throw, the farther it flies before it lands. If you threw it at a huge speed, almost eight kilometers per second, the ground beneath the stone would curve away as fast as it falls, and the stone would circle the planet. This thought experiment was invented by Isaac Newton, long before the first rockets. The higher the orbit, the slower a satellite can travel. Low satellites, like the space station, circle the Earth in an hour and a half. Communication satellites are much higher, about thirty-six thousand kilometers up. There it takes them exactly one day to go around the Earth, so they seem to hang motionless over one point on the equator. That is where satellite TV dishes are pointed. Satellites do not fly entirely without resistance: even very high up there is a little air that gradually slows them down. So stations fire their engines from time to time to raise their orbit. Old satellites that have served their time are either moved higher, into a graveyard orbit, or sent down into the atmosphere, where they burn up. Thousands of satellites now circle the Earth, and engineers watch carefully so that they don't collide with each other or with debris. Every time you check the weather forecast or use a navigation app, these forever-falling machines are helping you. And if on a clear night you see a little star moving slowly and steadily across the sky without twinkling, it is most likely a satellite, or even the space station, where people are working right now.

My first overnight hike happened when I was twelve. Dad, his friend and I planned to walk for two days along the river to an old mill and back. The evening before, I repacked my backpack three times: sleeping bag, flashlight, spare socks, a mug, sandwiches and a book I never even opened. Dad looked at my backpack, took out half the things and said that on a hike the point is not to take everything but to take what you need. We set off early in the morning, while mist still hung over the river. For the first few hours I walked briskly and kept running ahead. By lunchtime my legs were heavy, and the backpack, though half empty, felt as if it were full of stones. Dad suggested a rest every hour, and during the breaks we drank tea from a thermos, watched the water and listened to the birds. His friend knew the songs of almost every bird and taught me to tell a chaffinch from a great tit. In the evening we pitched the tent in a clearing at a bend in the river. For the first time I helped light a campfire and found it is not nearly as easy as in the movies: first you need thin dry twigs, then thicker ones, and only then logs. Our supper of buckwheat with canned beef seemed like the tastiest meal of my life. That night I couldn't fall asleep for a long time: something was rustling outside, some bird was calling, and above the tent there were more stars than I had ever seen in the city. In the morning it turned out the rustling was a hedgehog trying to get at our food. We reached the mill by noon: it had no roof, but the wheel was still in place. I came home tired, with blisters, and completely happy. Since then we have gone hiking every summer, and now I pack my backpack myself. And we call that clearing by the river bend our own.

Why does a year have 365 days, and not a round 360 or 400? It all comes down to how the Earth moves around the Sun. One full orbit takes about 365 days and almost six hours more. Ancient people noticed this long before telescopes: they watched where the sun rose, how the length of the day changed and when the rivers flooded. The Egyptians, for example, waited for the Nile to flood and noticed that it came soon after the bright star Sirius first appeared at dawn. But those extra six hours cause trouble. If every year is counted as exactly 365 days, the calendar slowly drifts away from the sun, and after a few centuries winter would fall in the summer months. To prevent this, ancient Rome under Julius Caesar introduced the leap year: once every four years an extra day is added to February. But even that was not quite enough. The year is actually a little shorter than 365 days and six hours, and over the centuries extra days piled up. By the sixteenth century the difference had reached ten days, and Pope Gregory XIII carried out a reform. Ten days were simply removed from the calendar: October 4 was followed directly by October 15. And so that the error would not build up again, it was decided that years divisible by 100 would not be leap years unless they were also divisible by 400. That is why 2000 was a leap year, but 1900 was not. Different countries switched to the new calendar at different times. Russia did so only in 1918, which is why some old holidays there are still celebrated by the Old Style calendar. Even today's calendar is not perfect: in about three thousand years it will be off by a whole day. But by then people will probably have come up with something new.
